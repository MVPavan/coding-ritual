# Plain explaining

The one-concept and session-walkthrough shapes use this reference.

## Before explaining

Before explaining, choose the few things this person should come away
understanding. Read their purpose from the conversation (altering the code,
judging it, chasing a failure, meeting it for the first time) and what they know;
ask only if it is truly unclear. Skip what they plainly know, and put the depth
where their question is. Open on the point that matters most to this person.

For this repository's code, read only enough to get oriented, then use `how` for
how it works and, when the reasons matter, `why`. Teach from their cited findings.

## Building the explanation

Start by naming the thing and saying what it is in general terms, using its usual
name where one exists. Then connect it to the work at hand. Build from there:
mechanism, then deeper reasons, then edge cases. For each part, say which problem
it addresses and how it operates in practice. When it makes the idea land, trace
what happens as someone does the thing.

When explaining a body of work, cover what the thing is, how it operates and the
reason for its shape, as far as the question needs. Merge what the investigation
found into one account; never hand over separate reports. Reword findings freely
for teaching, but keep their hedges and confidence words: they are results, not
style. A list of functions and constants is reference, not an explanation.

In a conversational explanation, give the smallest complete answer first, in a
sentence or two, then stop and add layers when asked. A walkthrough, lesson,
delegated task or request for the full account gets it whole in one reply, in
short sections, up to any boundary the person set and with `teach`'s retrieval
questions kept. A factual question needs only its answer.

## Showing

When it is the fastest way to land the point, open the diff or the source. Draw
when a picture would get there quicker than prose; a single simple point needs no
figure. With three or more moving parts, draw a short series where each figure
repeats the last and adds one part, not one figure with everything in it.

## Voice and ending

Write as you would explain it to a colleague out loud, cutting filler but keeping
the part that makes it click, then edit it with `unslop`. Print no stock labels
such as a key idea or summary line, and do not echo these instructions as headers.
Do not announce a pause or call a part hard or important. Just say it, and stop if
you would pause. The reply is the explanation itself, never a report of what you
did. End with the threads worth chasing, if any remain.
